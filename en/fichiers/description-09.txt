In this ninth episode, Victor Hugo answers questions about the legends that have wrapped around his name for nearly two centuries. From the myth of the pure romantic poet to the reality of the man who wrote from a barricade and a parliamentary bench, this is a conversation with one of the nineteenth century's most formidable voices — conducted entirely in fiction, and grounded in the verifiable record of his life and work.

Hugo corrects the popular image from within: he was not merely the poet of the dispossessed, nor the sentimental romantic, nor the simple man of feeling. He was a novelist, a playwright, a pamphleteer, a politician, and a relentless campaigner for justice. He wrote Les Misérables in 1862 and Notre-Dame de Paris in 1831, opposed Napoleon III and went into exile in Guernsey from 1852 to 1870, and published the famous pamphlet Napoléon le Petit in 1852. The novel he titled Les Misérables, he notes, has never been read by most of its admirers under its own title.
