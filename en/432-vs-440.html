Before 1859: a harmonious chaos

For centuries, no tuning standard existed. Handel traveled with his own pitch (~423 Hz), Mozart with his (~422). Every city, every church, every opera tuned its own way — singers wore out prematurely as orchestras crept higher. In 1859, France fixed the first standard: 435 Hz, the diapason normal.

Verdi and the short-lived 432

Worried about singers' voices, Verdi embraced the "scientific pitch" of physicist Joseph Sauveur (a reference C at 256 Hz — a power of two), championed by Charles Meerens: an A at 432 Hz, from the Pythagorean ratio 27/16. In 1881, a congress of Italian musicians in Milan adopted it; in 1884, the Italian Ministry of War imposed it on military bands. An official Italian standard… abandoned as early as 1885 at the Vienna congress. The irony: today's defenders of 432 claim ancient Egyptian wisdom — it was a nineteenth-century physicists' proposal.

440: not a plot, an engineers' convention

440 was already common in Holland in the 17th century. The London conference of 1939 — built around the radio industry — recommended it; ISO 16 (1955), confirmed in 1975, enshrined it. No trace of the "Nazi imposition" of the legend: a practical convention for worldwide exchange.

The real studies — and their nuance

Real pilot studies exist: an Italian double-blind trial (2019) where heart rate dropped more at 432; a study of emergency nurses during the pandemic where anxiety fell… in all three groups, including the one with no music at all. These are pilots: small samples, modest effects, never replicated. With a gap between 432 and 440 under a sixth of a tone, a massive physiological effect is very unlikely. The soothing comes from the break, the music, the ritual — not the number.

The tuning fork is neither a weapon nor a promise: it is an agreement between human beings.
